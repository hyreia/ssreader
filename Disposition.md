# DISPOSITION

## Act 1. Good Money

By the time I found the job at Disposition Services, I had stopped being particular about what kind of work I was willing to do. 
 Three months without a steady paycheck has a way of simplifying your standards.

I had applied for warehouse jobs, delivery jobs, night-shift jobs, customer service jobs, and one position cleaning office buildings that wanted two years of commercial cleaning experience.

Then, I found Disposition Services. The job title was “Inventory Processing Associate”.

The description was almost insultingly simple: Receive incoming materials. Inspect and catalog items. Maintain digital records. Prepare materials for storage. Follow confidentiality procedures.

No experience required. Forty dollars an hour.

I read the listing twice. Then a third time, because I assumed I had missed something.

I hadn't.

The interview lasted five minutes. The woman interviewing me, Karen, didn’t even look at my resume.

She spent most of it explaining that the company handled sensitive personal property and that discretion was extremely important.

"Are you comfortable working independently?" 

she asked.

"Yes."
"Are you comfortable handling personal effects?"
"Yes."
"Some of the materials can be emotionally difficult."

I hesitated.

"I think I'll be okay."

She nodded as though that were the answer she expected.

"Good. We can start you Monday."

That was it.

No drug test. No personality assessment. No three interviews with increasingly important people. No ridiculous online questionnaire asking me what kind of tree I would be.

Monday morning, I showed up twenty minutes early.

It looked like a warehouse.

That was probably the strangest thing about it.

Given the pay and the vague job description, I had expected something more unusual. Instead, the building sat in an industrial park between a plumbing supply company and a business that manufactured plastic components.

There was a loading dock, a parking lot, an employee entrance with a badge scanner and a customer entrance.

By ten-thirty, I'd signed my paperwork, watched an hour of training videos, and been given a locker.

By lunch, I'd met six other employees. Nobody seemed unusual. Kevin in the warehouse complained about the vending machines. Carl spent ten minutes explaining why the manager had been wrong about the new inventory software. Sarah asked me whether I'd found a place to live yet. I actually had just found a new place to live. She probably assumed I was just out of my parents’.

It was, in every respect, a normal job. Just a very well-paying one.

My supervisor, Karen, gave me a tour after lunch.

"Most of what comes in here is straightforward," 

she said.

We passed through a large receiving area where cardboard boxes sat on pallets.

"People send things directly to us. Sometimes they're brought in by courier. Sometimes we arrange pickup, and sometimes they bring them in themselves."

I looked at the boxes.

"What's in them?"

"Anything." She said it casually. 

"Personal property. Documents. Photographs. Things like that."

"Like estate stuff?"

"Sometimes."

We stopped at a workstation. It was my workstation now. There was a computer, barcode scanner, digital camera, gloves, and a stack of blank inventory forms.

Karen put a thin folder on the desk.

"Your job is to make sure everything gets identified, photographed, entered into the system, and stored in the correct location."

I opened the folder.

The first page was a checklist: Condition, Description, Source, Associated documentation, Disposition category

"What's 'disposition category'?"

"That's the part you'll learn."

She smiled.

"Don't worry about it yet."

I looked around the warehouse. Rows of shelving stretched into the distance. There were tens of thousands of boxes. Some were labeled. Some weren't. A few shelves contained things that weren't boxed at all: I saw framed photographs. A bicycle. A child's plastic wagon. Several sealed file boxes. A guitar. An old television. Nothing particularly strange.

Karen handed me a pair of gloves.

"One other thing." 

I looked up. 

"Confidentiality."

"Obviously."

"I mean it,” she warned. Her tone had changed slightly. 

"You'll see personal information. Medical records. Legal documents. Photographs. Things people aren't going to want other people seeing."

"Understood."

"Only access what you need to do your job."

I nodded.

"Got it."

She pointed toward a door at the end of the aisle.

"That section is restricted. You'll be trained on it later."

"What's back there?"

"More sensitive material."

"Okay."

Karen looked at me for a moment. Then she smiled again. 

"Welcome aboard."

<break>

My first three days were boring.

I liked that.

I'd forgotten how much I liked boring.

I worked at my computer, processed incoming material, scanned documents, photographed objects, and moved everything into storage.

The job required concentration but almost no interaction with other people. Exactly what I'd wanted. And the money was real. My first paycheck hit my account on Friday.

I stared at the number for several seconds. It wasn't a mistake. 

I could breathe again.

That afternoon, I was manning the counter for the first time when a woman came in carrying a shoebox. She looked to be in her sixties. She placed the box on the counter and kept both hands resting on the lid.

"Do I need to explain everything?" 

she asked.

"No. Just enough for us to identify the materials and process the transfer." 

I had practiced that line.

She nodded.

Then she opened the box.

There were photographs inside. Hundreds.

Family photographs, mostly: birthdays, Christmases, children, vacations, a wedding.

The woman across from me stood very still while I inspected the contents. We didn't say anything.

I started categorizing the contents and getting a rough estimate of how much of everything there was. Mostly photographs and knicknacks that looked like tacky souvenirs.

"I'm sorry," 
the customer finally said. 

"I thought I could do this."
I looked up.

She was crying.

I stopped working.

"Do you want a minute?"

She shook her head. 
"No. If I stop, I'll take them home."

I didn't know what to say to that. So I went back to work. 

When the last of the photographs had been cataloged, I entered the information into the computer. It told me where to store it and printed off the transfer form. 

The woman signed the transfer form.

I had to fetch a supervisor to unlock the safe. It was a sizable payment: hundreds of dollars for a lifetime of photographs, and we paid in cash. 

I handed her the money and she stared at it. Then at the box. Then back at the money.

Something changed in her expression. Not happiness. Relief.

She picked up her purse. 

"Thank you."

I nodded.

She walked toward the exit. At the door, she stopped. 

For one strange second, she looked confused. 

Then she smiled.

Not at me, at nothing. Then she left.

I watched her go.

I looked at the box of photographs. I wondered what had happened to her.

Then I picked it up and went to go store it away.

<break>	

The teddy bear arrived the following Tuesday: it was small, brown and touching it left an ashy residue on my gloves. Parts of it were black. The intake paperwork listed the owner as Margaret Ellis.

I was halfway through photographing it when I noticed the note attached to the file:

CUSTOMER REQUESTS NO FURTHER CONTACT REGARDING ASSOCIATED MATERIAL.

I frowned. There was another line beneath it:

DO NOT PROVIDE COPIES OF ASSOCIATED DOCUMENTATION.

That seemed odd. I looked toward Karen's office. She was on the phone. 

I turned back to the bear. I finished the photographs. Then I opened the next document. It was a medical report. I read the first paragraph. Then the second. I stopped.

The report described a child. A little girl. Seven years old. I read another line. Then another.

My stomach tightened.

I kept reading.

The girl had been admitted to the hospital following a fire.

There were injuries, a long painful recovery, complications and then an extended period of unconsciousness. Then-

I stopped. I stared at the screen. The medical language was clinical.

Detached. It was supposed to be.

But I could suddenly picture the room anyway: the machines, the hospital bed, the little girl, the teddy bear maybe sitting beside her. I closed the file.

For a moment, I sat there with both hands flat against the desk. I hadn't realized I'd been holding my breath.

"Everything okay?"

I looked up. Karen stood behind me. I closed the browser window.
"Yeah."

She glanced at the teddy bear. Then at the screen. 

"Did you read that?"

I hesitated. 

"Just the report."

Karen sighed. Not angrily. Almost sympathetically. 
"You're not supposed to."

"I know."

"You've been here a week."

"I know."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Then she said, 
"You don't want to start doing that."

I looked at her. 
"Why?"

She considered the question. 
"Because it stays with you."

I looked back at the teddy bear.

"How do you know?"

Karen gave me a tired smile.

"Because everyone thinks they're different."

She eyed me carefully to make sure I understood: 
"Just catalog the item."

I nodded. Karen walked away.

I looked at the teddy bear again. It was delicate, practically crumbling into ash in places.

I turned back to my computer. I finished the entry. Then I put the teddy bear into its storage container. I sealed the lid.

And for the rest of the afternoon, I didn't open another file.

That night, I lay awake in bed. I kept thinking about the report. I tried watching television. I tried listening to music. Eventually I turned the lights off. Every time I closed my eyes, I saw the teddy bear. Then the hospital room. Then the line in the report I'd wished I hadn't read.

At three in the morning, I finally sat up. I rubbed my face. 
"This is stupid."

I'd read a medical report. That was all. People read horrible things every day. I wasn't a child. I could handle it. I turned on the television. The room filled with blue light.

I stared at the screen. And somewhere in the back of my mind, a thought quietly surfaced: There were thousands of things in that building; thousands of files; thousands of stories. I wondered what was in them.

I wondered what people had been trying to forget.

## Act 2. The Things People Want To Forget

I wish I could say that I listened to Karen.

I didn't.

The next day, I read another file.

It wasn't even particularly interesting. A man had surrendered a collection of photographs and several pieces of correspondence after his wife died. The photographs themselves weren't unusual. The correspondence was worse. There were years of letters between them, and the last few had been written while she was in hospice.

I skimmed all of them. 

I told myself it was because I wanted to understand what the company actually did. That was partly true. The fact that people were willing to hand over entire portions of their lives bothered me.

Mostly, though, I just wanted to know.

The next file was about a car accident.

The one after that was a homicide.

Then sometimes there were pages that looked like they had been torn from notebooks. I stopped halfway through that one. It was a dairy entry of someone’s worst day of their life. Based on the handwriting, it was a mother describing the sound her son made as he died.

A woman who had found her husband in the bathtub and had never been able to take a bath herself afterward.

I began mentally carrying those things home with me.

I'd see someone sitting alone in a restaurant and wonder what terrible thing had happened to them. I'd hear an ambulance at night and think about one of the reports.

Once, I was driving home and passed a house with a child's bicycle lying in the yard. I had to pull over because I suddenly remembered a photograph from work.

I hadn't known the people in the photograph. I didn't know their names. I didn't know where they lived. I'd never met them.

But I knew what had happened to their child. That was when I started to understand what Karen meant: it stays with you.

Finally, I had to tell myself to stop. It was becoming too much for me. No more speculating on photographs. No more reading police reports. No more diary pages. 

Every morning, I walked into work and trained myself not to look: no more red folders, no more photographs, no more imagining what had happened to the people behind them. 

And for a while that worked. 

Until I got better at my job and started taking in a new disposition category: Aberrant.



I didn't realize that was what they were at first. I had started getting better at not reading or really taking in the photographs.

There was just a sense that something was off about them. The associated documentation was unusually extensive. That’s what really slowed me down: there was so much to document for one entry. Sometimes there would be photographs included that didn't seem to correspond to anything in particular or of anyone in particular. Just shots of nature. I promised myself I wouldn’t read them or look too closely.

But one afternoon, a new special warning caught my eye: 

SUBJECT REPORTS PHYSICAL CONTACT WITH ABERRANT.

I stared at it for a while. That wasn't the sort of thing I'd expected to find.

I opened the associated report. It was a testimony.

A man described going hunting with his brother in northern Michigan. He'd gotten separated from him after dark and spent several hours trying to find his way back to camp.

At some point, he heard something moving through the trees. He assumed it was a deer. Then it stood up.

The description went on for several pages.

I read the whole thing. The man was convinced he'd seen something that wasn't animal or human. He'd taken three photographs while trying to use the flash to find his way in the dark.

The first was mostly darkness. The second showed trees. The third showed something pale between them.

I stared at the photograph. It was blurry so I couldn't make out what I was looking at.

It might have been a person playing a prank. It might have been a trick of the light. It might have been nothing.

I closed the file, finished cataloging, and the computer spat out where to store it. I filed it all away: physically and mentally.

Later that day another aberrant disposition came in with the same warning:

SUBJECT REPORTS PHYSICAL CONTACT WITH ABERRANT.

I had to know so I read this one too.

A woman in Oregon had been driving home late at night when she saw something in the road.

She described it as tall. She said it had moved strangely, as though it had forgotten how its body was supposed to work.

She had gotten out of her car. At first she thought it was a man. Then she got closer and realized how tall it was.

When she saw its face she realized it wasn’t a man.

She had barely gotten back into her car and floored it to get away.

She had spent the next six months unable to sleep.

Then, somehow the photographs and the police report ended up at Disposition Services. The source on the paperwork was blank. I had seen that before and was told that was okay but it still made me wonder who had brought this in then: the police?

I documented everything, submitted it and my computer gave me the department, row, section and shelf numbers. I went to put away the box in the aberrant department of the warehouse. 

Something I hadn’t noticed until months into the job of putting the boxes away was that things weren’t filed away arbitrarily. They were put with related cases. One of the first morbid sections I memorized was child death.

Here in the aberrant department, holding the box for the tall thing the lady had seen, I saw that it was the same case here: there were dozens of boxes with nearly identical numbers.



I kept my head down for the next few months and worked as quickly as I could on everything else. I no longer cared about any of the other cases. My work metrics soared and I was praised for it. Karen thought I'd finally stopped reading cases. 

She was wrong though. I was just being more selective: I wanted time to read the aberrant cases when they ended up on my desk.

For the next several months: I read about things in forests, things beside roads that should have been dead, things watching people inside their homes. One box came in with twenty-three eyewitness accounts of a *thing* eating someone in public. The victim’s identification came in the box with them: a social security card, a driver’s license, a birth certificate, an autopsy report, even a fifth grade report card.

There were sections on people claiming to have seen Bigfoot, skinwalkers, demons, angels, even extraterrestrials.

I started searching random aberent numbers on the computer. Some of them had dates going back decades. One had been entered in 1978\. Another in 1954\.

I even managed to find handwritten inventory forms scanned into our system from the 40s.

I stared at the screen.

The company had been collecting these stories for a very long time.

## Act 3. The Collection

The next morning, I started looking for the older boxes.

I told myself I was only curious.

I wasn't going to read anything. I wasn't going to look at the photographs. I wasn't going to search for new cases.

I just wanted to know what had happened to the old ones.

I started with the 1978 case.

The database still had it. I found the box number and went into the warehouse.

The shelf was empty.

I checked the number again.

Then the shelf beside it.

Then the one above it.

Nothing.

I went back to the computer and pulled up the record again, expecting to find some note about it being moved.

There was a field I hadn't taken into consideration before:

DISPOSITION: COLLECTION

I stared at it.

Then I searched for the 1954 case.

Same thing.

The Oregon case.

Same.

I searched for a recent case I remembered cataloging only a few months earlier: Margaret Ellis's Teddy Bear.

Gone.

DISPOSITION: COLLECTION

I started searching randomly.

Some cases were still where they were supposed to be.

Others were gone.

Every missing case had been marked for collection. There was no shipping company,

no destination, no manifest, no signature. Just a date and a time.

I found another. Then another. They all had the same collection window.

03:00–04:00

I frowned at the screen.

I'd assumed collection was something that happened during normal business hours. A truck came by. Someone signed for the boxes. They left.

That was what collection meant. It had to be.

I searched the employee handbook: nothing. 

The training database: nothing.

I searched the word collection by itself. Thousands of results.

The database went back further than I had realized. Some entries had been imported from older systems. There were scanned forms from the eighties, handwritten inventory sheets from the seventies, and a few documents that looked older than the company itself.

The terminology changed over the years. The procedure didn't. Things were received, cataloged, stored. Then, eventually, they were collected.

The collection records never listed a recipient: there was no destination, there wasn't even a field for one. There was only a client acknowledgement. I searched for it. All the collections came back:

CLIENT ACKNOWLEDGEMENT: RECEIVED

That was all: no name, no address, no company, nothing.

I opened another record. Same thing. Another. Every collection ended with:

CLIENT ACKNOWLEDGEMENT: RECEIVED

I sat there for a long time. Then I looked at the clock.

I'd been searching for almost an hour. For the first time, I wondered if there was something I wasn't supposed to know. That thought should have made me stop.

Instead, I searched for PAYMENT STATUS.

There were three results.

PENDING.

FAILED.

COMPLETE.

I opened a completed record.

There was a payment amount.

I blinked.

Two payment amounts: the one we paid the seller and an amount received.

PAYMENT STATUS: COMPLETE

I sat back.

And finally, it made sense.

We weren't a storage company.

We weren't keeping these things.

We were selling them.

I looked toward the warehouse.

All those shelves.

All those boxes.

All those photographs and reports and little pieces of people's lives.

For the first time, they didn't look like an archive.

They looked like inventory.

I looked at the collection window again.

03:00–04:00

I'd never worked that late.

I closed the database and forgot about it. I went back to filing diary entries and knicknacks and wondered who would want these.

Eventually it was time to go home and I quietly left. 

I told myself I was going to forget about it.

I didn't.

At around eleven, I went to bed.

At 2:15 in the morning, I woke up.

There was no reason for it.

I had no alarm set.

For several seconds, I just stared at the ceiling.

Then I looked at the clock.

2:15.

I got up.

I got dressed.

I even made breakfast.

I remember standing in my kitchen eating toast and thinking that this was probably the stupidest thing I'd ever done.

At 2:47, I was sitting across the street from the warehouse.

I hadn't parked in the employee lot.

I turned off the engine.

Then the headlights.

Then everything else.

The building was mostly dark.

A few lights were on inside. The security lights over the loading dock were the brightest.

I watched the clock.

2:55.

A light came on inside the warehouse.

2:57.

Someone walked across the loading area.

I recognized him, it was Kevin in receiving just holding a clipboard.

At 2:59, he unlocked the loading door. Then he stood there. Waiting.

At exactly 3:00, he looked at his watch.

Nothing happened. He didn't seem confused. He didn't seem nervous.

He just waited. I started wondering if I'd misunderstood the records. Maybe collection didn't happen every night. Maybe the time was the beginning of a window.

Maybe a truck was coming. I waited another minute.

Then another.

At 3:03, the receiving employee stepped back.

He looked toward the parking lot. I followed his gaze: there was nothing there. No truck. No car. No person. Nothing.

The warehouse lights continued humming overhead. But I knew from the way Kevin was acting that something had changed. 

He wasn’t waiting anymore. He looked down at his clipboard. Checked something. Then he walked back inside.

I opened my car door. I don't know why. I think some part of me still believed there had to be an explanation.

Either way, I crossed the street on foot and was drawn to the warehouse light like a moth to a flame.

I moved along the side of the building until I could see through the loading bay. The lone employee was standing beside a pallet.

There were six boxes on it. I recognized some of the labels.

Aberrant. Photographs were sticking out of one of the containers.

Another had a red folder sitting on top. The employee checked his clipboard.

Something blocked the light.

I stopped. I tried to understand what I was seeing.

My mind reached for a shape. It couldn't hold one.

Something like a body. No. Something like a person. No. A mass.

I could see things where legs should have been, except they weren't really legs. Something occupied the place where a torso should have been. Something extended from it. Or several somethings. Parts of its mass oscillated and spun.

Was it on fire?

When I looked deeper into it, I saw things that felt like eyes looking back at me. Far too many eyes for any creature to need.

I felt them.

It didn’t react to my presence, it didn’t move from whatever it was doing. And yet, I somehow knew it could see me because I could see it. 

Kevin stepped aside for it. The thing moved toward the pallet.

It didn't reach for the boxes. At least, I don't think it did.

I couldn't see a hand. I couldn't see anything touch them.

The first box was simply gone. Then the second.

Then the third. Each time, there was a sound.

Cardboard scraping against concrete. But nothing was moving the boxes.

They were there. Then they weren't. Six boxes. Gone.

The employee made a mark on his clipboard. He looked toward the thing.

Then toward the loading door.

Everything about him suggested he was finishing a routine task.

I realized I was holding my breath.

Then whatever it was turned toward me. I know that's what happened but I don't know how I know. There was no face or a ‘front’. Nothing I could point to but I knew it was now directed at me.

Some primal instinct kicked in and I began running away before I realized it. I don't remember turning around.

I don't remember crossing the street. I only remember running.

There was screaming. Animalistic, feral screaming. The worst screaming I'd ever heard. My vision swam with black spots.

I ran faster. The sound followed me.

Footsteps. There were human footsteps.

My vision began to blur. I couldn't see where I was going. I only cared about getting distance. 

The pavement seemed to tilt beneath me and I fell.

I hit the pavement hard enough to knock the air out of me. The screaming was punctured and went silent.

I tried to crawl. I tried to call for help but I was too winded. Something was coming closer.

I fought it. I kicked. I twisted around. A hand went over my mouth. A human hand. 

“Stop screaming.”

## Act 4. The Exit

“Stop screaming.”

It took me a second to recognize the voice. Kevin was kneeling beside me. His hand was still over my mouth.

I tried to pull away from him.

“It's gone,” he said.

I looked back toward the warehouse.

The loading door was still open.

The pallet was empty.

There was nothing in the doorway.

Nothing in the parking lot.

Nothing anywhere that I could see.

He removed his hand from my mouth. I was still shaking.

“What was that?”

“A Buyer.”

“But what the hell is it???”

“I don't know,” he admitted. He helped me sit up.

“What do you mean you don’t know???”

“You need to go home.”

“I saw-”

“I know what you saw.”

“No, you don't.”

After a moment he replied, “I suppose neither of us know.”

We sat there for a while.

I don't remember how long.

Eventually Kevin helped me back to my car. He didn't ask me why I had come. I didn't ask him why he was still working at three in the morning.

I drove home. Fearful something would be waiting for me at home. I turned on all the lights, turned on everything that made noise. I took a cold shower.

I didn't sleep. I couldn't sleep, not when I couldn’t trust the shadows in my apartment anymore. I didn’t leave the apartment. I just needed to be safe, to block out my thoughts with anything I could find. 

I didn't go to work the next day.

Or the day after that.

On the third morning, there was a knock on my apartment door and I saw it was our boss, Karen. She stood outside my door holding two cups of coffee in a carrying case in one hand and a box with a folder on top under the other.

“Two no-call no-shows is automatic termination,” she said through the door.

“How do you know where I live?” I called out.

“Employee paperwork.”

“...Oh, right.”

A part of me wanted her here, so I opened the door. She always seemed to be in control, and that’s what I needed right now. 

She held out the coffees. I took one, almost robotically. I was in a daze from the lack of sleep. I was defeated but also felt a sense of hope that this nightmare was coming to an end now that she was here.

“Can I come in?”

I let her. Something about her calm demeanor was welcoming to my three days of hiding in my apartment, trying to distract myself from what I knew. 

She walked in and didn’t look around at all. She walked to the kitchen table and sat down in the chair guests sit in. I sat down across from her. She sat the box, the kind we used for inventory, on the table and the folder down next to it.

“So, let’s talk about what happened,” Karen offered.

“I saw one,” I said.

Karen nodded.

“What are they?” I asked.

She took a sip of coffee.

“Buyers.”

I stared at her.

“That isn't an answer.”

“We don’t ask questions,” she countered.

“What does that mean?”

Karen set her coffee down.

“You found the collection records.”

“Yes.”

“And you saw a collection occur.”

“Yes.”

She nodded.

“Okay.”

“Okay?”

“You're going to need to understand something about Disposition Services.”

I waited.

“People come to us because they want their trauma gone. Sometimes it's attached to an object. Sometimes it's photographs. Sometimes it's records. Sometimes it's everything they have left of someone. They want it gone and everything attached to it.”

“And people just forget? Completely? How?”

“It’s part of the contract they sign,” Karen explained matter of factly.

I thought of the burnt teddy bear.

“This is insane. So we're just selling people's worst memories to things we don't understand?”

“That's one way to put it.”

“What other way is there?”

“The company buys something people desperately want to get rid of. We give them money for it. Someone else wants what we've bought. We sell it to them.” She shrugged. “That's the business.”

I stared at her.

“That doesn't bother you?”

“Not anymore.”

“Why?”

“We are doing a good thing: the worst memories, the things that prevent people from living, the things that destroy people and prevent them from moving on, we buy them. We give people money and they get to forget.”

I thought about all the things I'd read.

The photographs I'd seen. The reports. The grisly accidents. The family tragedies. The worst acts of humanity. Encounters with things that shouldn't exist-the monsters that live just beyond normalcy. The aberrants. 

“What do they do with it?”

Karen shrugged. “I don't know.”

“Why do they want it?”

“I don't know.”

“Then why would you sell it to them?”

She looked genuinely confused by the question. “Why wouldn't we? People want to get rid of it and they want to buy it.”

I had nothing to say to that.

Karen picked up her coffee. “You've quit.”

It wasn't a question.

“Yes.”

She nodded. “Okay.”

That's when I started crying.

Not because she was being kind, because I didn’t know what to do now. I wiped my face.

“I just can't work there anymore.”

“I understand.”

“I wish I didn’t know what I know.”

“You won't.”

“What?”

I looked at her. She didn't answer immediately. She opened the folder and set a form on the table.

“There's an exit agreement.”

I stared at it.

“What is that?”

“It closes your employment.”

“I already quit.”

“Yes, but consider this an optional severance package.”

She tapped the page.

“You sell your memories of the job to us. Everything you’ve read, every photograph you looked at, seeing a Buyer. You sell all of that trauma back to us and then you can sleep again.”

I laughed. “You're serious.”

Karen nodded. “Everything associated with your employment with us will be removed.”

“And I get paid?”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

She told me.

I stared at her.

It was basically a week’s pay there.

“You pay people to forget working there?”

“We pay people for what they want gone.”

“...does this happen a lot? With employees, I mean.”

“It’s very common. Yes.”

I looked at the box she brought in then back at Karen.

“Is that where you put them?”

She opened it and I flinched instinctively to cover my eyes.

“Actually, this one is just for you.”

I looked back at her then down into the box.

One last time, I snooped through a record.

There was a termination form. There was a payment authorization. There was a confidentiality agreement. 

Underneath the papers were badges. I stared at them. They all had my name on them.

I looked at Karen.

“I don't remember any of this.”

“I know.”

“How many times?”

She pointed at my unsigned termination form on the table.

“This will be your fifth.”

“...Why do I keep coming back?”

She gave me a small, sad smile.

“You keep needing a job.”

“And you rehire me???”

“You catch on fast.”

EMPLOYEE ACKNOWLEDGES VOLUNTARY DISPOSITION OF EMPLOYMENT-RELATED MEMORIES.

I signed it.

I remember signing it.

I remember Karen taking the papers.

I remember her telling me I would feel better soon.

I remember the money appearing in my account.

After that, there was nothing.

I just knew that I was let go.

There was a weird time gap in my resume that didn't make sense, so I adjusted the dates before I got another job.

But I was bad at finding stable income.

Later that year, I was back to job hunting again.

I was sitting at my computer looking through job listings when I saw it.

“Inventory Processing Associate”.

Receive incoming materials. Inspect and catalog items. Maintain digital records. Prepare materials for storage. Follow confidentiality procedures.

No experience required. Forty dollars an hour.

Disposition Services.

I stared at the screen for a long time.

Then I clicked Apply.
